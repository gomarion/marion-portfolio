*After years of working with content management systems, I finally
decided to explore the modern JavaScript frameworks I'd been wanting to
work with for a long time.*

For most of my career, I've worked with platforms that made building and
managing websites relatively straightforward. WordPress, Shopify, Ghost,
and Tumblr have been a significant part of my [development journey](/#experience),
particularly when it comes to theme development and customization.

I've spent years working with these platforms, understanding their
ecosystems, building custom themes, troubleshooting issues, and finding
ways to extend their capabilities.

But for the longest time, there was something else I wanted to explore:
modern JavaScript frameworks like <a href="https://react.dev/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" className="text-link underline underline-offset-4 transition-opacity hover:opacity-70">React</a> and <a href="https://nextjs.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" className="text-link underline underline-offset-4 transition-opacity hover:opacity-70">Next.js</a>.

It wasn't that I didn't have an interest in learning them. I simply
never had the opportunity to focus on them professionally. In my
previous roles, particularly as a [Senior Web Developer](/#experience), much of my time
was dedicated to managing projects, coordinating development work,
handling technical responsibilities, and taking care of other things
that came with the role.

Eventually, I found myself in a position where I could finally give
these technologies the attention I wanted to give them.

When I decided to recreate my original WordPress portfolio into
something more developer-oriented, I thought it would be the perfect
opportunity. Rather than simply showcasing my experience with different
platforms, I wanted to demonstrate my interest in modern web development
by actually building something with the technologies I wanted to
explore.

That decision eventually led me to React, Next.js, and Vercel.

## The Comfort of Working with WordPress

WordPress has been a big part of my professional life.

Over the years, I've worked on custom themes, child themes, page
builders, plugins, WooCommerce implementations, and various other
customizations.

One of the things I appreciate about WordPress is its flexibility.
Depending on the project, you can start with an existing theme, build
something entirely custom, extend functionality through plugins, or
develop your own solutions.

The ecosystem is extensive, and there's usually more than one way to
accomplish something.

I've also become familiar with the operational side of running WordPress
websites. Hosting environments, server configurations, performance
optimization, security, backups, plugin compatibility, and maintenance
are all part of the experience.

Of course, WordPress comes with its own challenges.

Plugin dependencies, compatibility issues, database performance,
security updates, and maintaining multiple websites can become
demanding, especially when managing several projects.

But these are challenges I've learned to work with over the years.

I don't necessarily see these experiences as something I need to leave
behind. They're still valuable, and WordPress remains a platform I'm
comfortable working with.

I simply wanted to expand my skill set and explore a different way of
building for the web.

## I Wasn't Exactly Starting from Scratch

Although React and Next.js were relatively new territory for me, I
wasn't entirely unfamiliar with the JavaScript development workflow.

During my time at [Artistic Influence](/#experience), I was already working with
Node.js, NPM, and JavaScript APIs as part of my theme development
workflow.

I used tools like <a href="https://webpack.js.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" className="text-link underline underline-offset-4 transition-opacity hover:opacity-70">Webpack</a> and <a href="https://vite.dev/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" className="text-link underline underline-offset-4 transition-opacity hover:opacity-70">ViteJS</a> to manage assets and build the
animations, styles, and interactive effects that went into our
commercial themes.

These tools were part of my day-to-day development process, particularly
when working on Shopify, Ghost, and Tumblr themes.

That experience gave me some familiarity with the tooling and workflows
commonly used in modern JavaScript development.

However, working with build tools and JavaScript APIs is quite different
from building an application using a framework like React or Next.js.

That's where things started getting interesting.

## Discovering React and Next.js

My interest in exploring modern JavaScript frameworks eventually brought
me to React.

Coming from a background where much of my work revolved around CMS
platforms and theme development, React introduced a different way of
thinking about building interfaces.

Instead of primarily working with templates, PHP, and platform-specific
structures, I started exploring component-based development.

The idea of building reusable components, managing application state,
and organizing interfaces into smaller, independent pieces was an
interesting shift.

Then came Next.js.

What caught my attention was how it builds on React by providing
additional capabilities and conventions for developing complete web
applications.

Routing, rendering strategies, server-side functionality, optimization,
and other features are integrated into the framework.

It wasn't simply another JavaScript library to learn. It introduced a
different set of architectural concepts and development workflows.

There was definitely a learning curve.

I had to become familiar with things like the App Router, Server
Components, Client Components, file-based routing, and how Next.js
handles rendering.

Some concepts felt intuitive, while others required more time and
experimentation.

And that's precisely what made the experience interesting.

## Building My Portfolio as a Learning Experience

Rather than just following tutorials or building isolated experiments, I
decided to put what I was learning into practice by developing my own
portfolio.

My original portfolio was built using WordPress, which served its
purpose well. But I eventually wanted something that better represented
my work as a developer, including the technologies I was currently
exploring.

I started by designing the website in Figma. I wanted to establish the
visual direction first, including the layout, typography, spacing, and
overall experience before moving into development.

For the implementation, I experimented with an AI-assisted workflow. I
used ChatGPT to research Next.js concepts, explore different approaches,
and better understand the technologies I was working with. I then used
Claude Code, feeding it my Figma designs as references and working
through the implementation to bring those designs to life.

It was an interesting experience because I wasn't just learning a new
framework. I was also learning how to work effectively with AI
development tools as part of an actual project.

Of course, it wasn't a matter of simply providing a design and getting a
finished website in return. There was plenty of back and forth,
reviewing generated code, making adjustments, troubleshooting issues,
and refining the implementation until things worked and looked the way I
intended.

This became my opportunity to explore the entire workflow, from setting
up a project to designing, developing, and deploying an actual website.

I also had to familiarize myself with some of the development
conventions and tools specific to the Next.js ecosystem.

Working with the App Router, organizing components, understanding
rendering strategies, and managing the different parts of a React
application introduced new concepts that I hadn't encountered in quite
the same way before.

Eventually, I connected the project to GitHub and deployed it through
Vercel.

Interestingly, the idea of connecting a GitHub repository to a
development environment wasn't entirely new to me. Back when I was
working on Shopify themes, I had already been using <a href="https://shopify.dev/docs/storefronts/themes/tools/github" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" className="text-link underline underline-offset-4 transition-opacity hover:opacity-70">Shopify's GitHub
integration</a>. It allowed me to connect a repository, have Shopify detect
new changes, and work with the latest updates on a development theme.

So, the concept of having a Git repository connected to a platform where
I could see and test my latest changes was already familiar.

What I found interesting about Vercel was how it extended that workflow
into the deployment of an entire application. By connecting my GitHub
repository, Vercel could automatically build and deploy the Next.js
application whenever I pushed new changes.

It was a familiar concept applied to a different development
environment. Instead of managing theme changes within Shopify, I was now
working with an application framework and a deployment platform that
handled much of the build and deployment process for me.

It gave me a better appreciation of how these modern development
workflows bring together source control, application builds, and
hosting.

The portfolio itself became more than just a place to showcase my work.
It became a project where I could experiment, make mistakes, and learn
something new along the way.

## A Different Way of Thinking About Websites

One of the biggest differences I've noticed is how much more control you
have over the underlying application structure when working with
Next.js.

With a CMS, much of the foundation is already established. You work
within the platform's conventions and extend its capabilities based on
the requirements of the project.

With Next.js, you're given a framework and a collection of tools, but
you have more responsibility for deciding how everything fits together.

You have to think about project architecture, component organization,
data fetching, rendering strategies, and how different parts of the
application communicate.

That flexibility is great, but it also means there are more decisions to
make.

I've also started appreciating concepts that weren't necessarily part of
my everyday workflow before.

For example, understanding the differences between server-side and
client-side rendering, learning how Server Components work, and
considering where application logic should live.

These aren't things you can fully understand by simply getting a website
to look right. They require a deeper understanding of how the framework
works.

It's a different kind of development experience, and I'm still learning
to navigate it.

## What About AI-Assisted Development?

AI-assisted development has become another interesting part of this
journey.

While working on my portfolio, I started incorporating tools like
ChatGPT and Claude Code into my development workflow.

I used ChatGPT primarily as a research companion. Whenever I encountered
unfamiliar concepts or needed to understand how something worked in
Next.js, I could explore the topic, ask questions, compare approaches,
and work through technical decisions.

Claude Code, on the other hand, became part of the actual implementation
process. I could provide it with my Figma designs, describe what I
wanted to build, and work through the generated code together.

This introduced a different way of approaching development. Instead of
writing every line of code myself, I was learning to communicate
requirements, provide context, review implementations, and guide the
tools toward the results I wanted.

But I've also realized that using AI effectively requires a different
kind of discipline.

It's easy to accept a generated solution simply because it appears to
work. The more important part is understanding what was generated,
reviewing the implementation, and making sure it actually fits the
project.

AI can help you move faster, but it doesn't eliminate the need to
understand what you're building.

If anything, it makes critical thinking and technical judgment even more
important.

## Am I Moving Away from WordPress?

Not at all.

I don't see this as a transition where one technology replaces another.

WordPress still has a place in my development toolkit, and my experience
with CMS platforms remains valuable.

Different projects have different requirements.

Some websites benefit from the flexibility and content management
capabilities of WordPress. Others may be better suited to a framework
like Next.js, particularly when there's a need for a more customized
application architecture or a particular development workflow.

I think the important thing is understanding the tools available and
knowing when to use them.

Having experience with different platforms also gives me a broader
perspective when approaching development problems.

Instead of being limited to one particular ecosystem, I can explore
different solutions and evaluate which approach makes the most sense for
a given project.

## Still Exploring, Still Learning

I'm still relatively new to the Next.js ecosystem compared to the years
I've spent working with WordPress and other CMS platforms.

There are plenty of things I haven't explored yet, and I'm sure I'll
encounter more challenges as I continue building.

But that's part of the experience.

I don't think developers should limit themselves to the technologies
they're already comfortable with. There's always something new worth
exploring, whether it's a framework, a development tool, a different
workflow, or an entirely new way of approaching a problem.

For me, exploring Next.js has been a reminder that experience doesn't
mean you stop being a beginner.

You can spend years building websites, become comfortable with your
tools, and still find yourself starting from scratch when you enter a
new ecosystem.

And there's nothing wrong with that.

Sometimes, the best way to grow as a developer is to step outside what
you already know and give yourself the opportunity to learn something
different.

This is just another chapter in my journey.

I'm still building, still experimenting, and still figuring things out.

And I intend to keep it that way.
